PE-22-28
the honest unknown

A seven-amino-acid peptide fragment with a Wikipedia page, one key preclinical paper, and almost nothing else verifiable. Vendors sell big stories about it — most of which evaporate on inspection. This page is about what little is actually known, and how to think about compounds like it.

Quick read

PE-22-28 (Gly-Val-Ser-Trp-Gly-Leu-Arg) is a heptapeptide fragment of spadin, itself derived from the sortilin propeptide, studied as a TREK-1 potassium-channel blocker in preclinical mood research. The verifiable record is thin: a 2017 pharmacology paper, a Wikipedia entry, chemical identifiers — and no human trials. Around that thin core orbits a fog of vendor marketing, including sellers who misidentify it as entirely different peptides. The honest story: an obscure research tool with an obscured information environment.

02Origin

The thin origin story

PE-22-28's history isn't a saga — it's a footnote that escaped into commerce. Here's the whole thing, honestly told.

From a 2017 paper to a fog of marketing in under a decade.

Spadin — the parent peptide — was studied as a naturally occurring TREK-1 blocker with fast-acting antidepressant-like effects in animals. PE-22-28 is a trimmed-down version chasing the same activity in a smaller package.

One paper, then commerce

The 2017 paper showed shortened analogs kept TREK-1 blocking activity with better stability. Then vendors moved faster than science — the compound entered catalogs long before any follow-up research.

With famous peptides, hype at least orbits real data. With obscure ones, there's no data gravity — marketing copy drifts wherever sellers want, unchecked by anything.

TREK-1 → neuronal excitability

Why does blocking a potassium channel change brain behavior? The proposed chain, in order — each step only follows the one before it:

1 · Channel blocked

PE-22-28 interacts with TREK-1 at the cell membrane. The channel's K⁺ leak current falls.

2 · Membrane shifts

Less K⁺ leaving means the membrane voltage drifts closer to the firing threshold. The neuron becomes easier to excite.

3 · Circuits respond

Changed excitability ripples through neural circuits — mood-related networks among them.

4 · Behavior, in animals

Rodent depression models show antidepressant-like effects. The last step in the chain — and the least proven in humans.

Serotonin, downstream

If serotonin-related effects ever come up, they sit downstream of the channel and circuit effects — there is no evidence PE-22-28 touches a serotonin receptor directly. PE-22-28 is not a serotonin or dopamine drug wearing a disguise (see the head-to-head chapter).

Plain English

The research story: block a calming potassium channel → brain cells get more excitable → mood-related circuits respond — in lab dishes and animals. It's a genuinely interesting neuroscience idea. It is not a human treatment, and the distance between those is measured in years and trials that don't exist.

10Glossary

Understanding PE-22-28 Terminology

Twelve terms, each with the science and a plain-English translation. Tap any term to expand it.

The scienceA heptapeptide with the amino-acid sequence Gly-Val-Ser-Trp-Gly-Leu-Arg, a fragment of the longer peptide spadin. It has been studied preclinically as a blocker of the TREK-1 potassium channel — the same proposed target as spadin itself. The published record is a single preclinical paper from 2017, and there are no human data: no trials, no safety profile, no approved use.

Plain EnglishPE-22-28 is a tiny seven-piece chain copied from a longer natural peptide called spadin. The one published study tested it in the lab as a blocker of a brain channel called TREK-1. That’s the entire scientific record — one paper, no human trials, no safety data. Everything beyond that is marketing, not knowledge.

The scienceA naturally occurring peptide derived from the propeptide of the protein sortilin, identified as a blocker of the TREK-1 potassium channel. In animal studies it produced antidepressant-like effects, and it is the parent molecule from which PE-22-28 was cut down. Unlike its fragment, spadin has an actual preclinical research record behind it. Research into spadin-family compounds remains confined to animals — none has entered human trials.

Plain EnglishSpadin is a natural peptide the body makes when it trims a larger protein called sortilin. Scientists found it blocks the TREK-1 brain channel and saw antidepressant-like effects in animals. PE-22-28 is a shortened copy of it. Spadin is the parent with a real (if animal-only) research history; the fragment is riding on that history.

The scienceA two-pore-domain potassium channel, encoded by the gene KCNK2, expressed widely in the brain. It is a “leak” channel: potassium ions flowing out through it keep the neuron’s membrane voltage steady and the cell calm. Blocking TREK-1 reduces that potassium outflow, which depolarizes the neuron and makes it more likely to fire. PE-22-28’s entire proposed mechanism is blocking this channel.

Plain EnglishTREK-1 is a channel in brain cells that lets potassium leak out — a kind of brake that keeps neurons calm and quiet. Block the channel and the brake lifts: the cell’s voltage rises and it fires more easily. This channel is the proposed target of PE-22-28. Note “proposed” — the evidence is one lab paper, not human biology.

The scienceA receptor and sorting protein involved in moving other proteins around inside cells. Its propeptide — the piece clipped off during the protein’s maturation — is the source of spadin. That makes sortilin the “grandparent” molecule in this lineage: sortilin yields spadin, and spadin yields PE-22-28.

Plain EnglishSortilin is a protein whose job is sorting other proteins inside cells. When the cell finishes building sortilin, it clips off a leftover piece — and that leftover is spadin. So sortilin is the grandparent, spadin the parent, and PE-22-28 the grandchild of this molecular family.

The scienceA peptide chain made of exactly seven amino acids linked in sequence. Seven residues is short enough to synthesize cheaply and reliably, which partly explains why such fragments are popular catalog items. PE-22-28’s heptapeptide identity — GVSWGLR — is one of the few verified facts about it.

Plain EnglishA heptapeptide is a protein fragment exactly seven building blocks long — “hepta” means seven. Short chains like this are cheap to manufacture, which is one reason vendors love them. That PE-22-28 is a heptapeptide is one of the few things about it that can actually be verified.

The scienceA molecule that binds to a receptor or channel without activating it, thereby blocking the natural signal. An antagonist occupies the target’s binding site like a key that fits the lock but won’t turn. PE-22-28 is described as a TREK-1 antagonist — it blocks the channel rather than stimulating anything.

Plain EnglishAn antagonist is a blocker, not a stimulator. It plugs into a receptor or channel and sits there, preventing the normal signal from getting through — like a key that fits the lock but won’t turn. That’s what “TREK-1 blocker” means: PE-22-28 is said to sit on the channel and keep it closed.

The scienceThe stage of research conducted before any human testing — experiments in cell cultures and animal models. Preclinical results can suggest a mechanism and flag obvious toxicity, but they cannot establish safety or efficacy in people. Every claimed effect of PE-22-28 lives at this stage, and nothing has moved beyond it.

Plain English“Preclinical” means research done before any human is ever involved — cells in dishes and animals in labs. It can hint at how something works, but it can’t prove it works or is safe in people. All of PE-22-28’s claimed effects sit at this stage. Nothing about it has ever been tested in a human being.

The scienceThe formation of new neurons from neural stem cells, occurring mainly in the hippocampus in the adult brain. Compounds in the spadin lineage have been reported to promote neurogenesis in animals — a finding sometimes cited as the basis for antidepressant-like effects. No human neurogenesis data exist for spadin, let alone for PE-22-28.

Plain EnglishNeurogenesis means growing brand-new brain cells — something the adult brain does in only a few regions, mainly the hippocampus. Animal studies reported that spadin-family compounds encouraged this growth, which is part of their appeal. But “shown in animals” is the whole story; there is zero human evidence, for spadin or for PE-22-28.

The scienceA shift in a neuron’s membrane voltage toward a less negative value — from its resting state of roughly −70 millivolts toward the firing threshold. Depolarization is the electrical step that makes a neuron more likely to fire an action potential. Blocking TREK-1 causes depolarization by reducing the potassium outflow that normally holds the voltage steady.

Plain EnglishA neuron at rest sits at a negative voltage, like a drawn bow. Depolarization is letting that voltage rise toward the firing point — the cell waking up and getting ready to send a signal. Blocking TREK-1 causes exactly this: with less potassium leaking out, the voltage climbs. It’s the electrical consequence of the proposed mechanism.

The scienceA unique numerical identifier assigned to a chemical substance by the Chemical Abstracts Service. Because names and abbreviations vary, the CAS number is the closest thing chemistry has to a fingerprint for confirming a compound’s identity. PE-22-28’s listed CAS number is 1801959-12-5 — useful in theory for checking what a vial actually contains.

Plain EnglishA CAS number is a chemical’s ID number — unique to that substance, assigned by the Chemical Abstracts Service. Names and nicknames can be fuzzy, but the number is supposed to be exact. PE-22-28’s listed number is 1801959-12-5. In theory it’s how you’d verify a vial’s claim; in an unregulated market, “in theory” does a lot of work.

The scienceA marketing pattern in which a derivative compound is sold on the strength of its parent’s research record. PE-22-28’s appeal leans heavily on spadin’s animal studies, even though a seven-amino-acid fragment is not the same molecule and inherits none of the data automatically. The halo belongs to spadin; the fragment only borrows it.

Plain EnglishA “borrowed halo” is when a knockoff product takes credit for the original’s good reputation. PE-22-28 gets its glow from spadin’s animal studies — but a shortened fragment is a different molecule, and research doesn’t transfer by association.
